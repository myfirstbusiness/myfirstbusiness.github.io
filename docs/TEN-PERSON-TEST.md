# The Ten-Person Test

**The highest-value thing you can do this month. It costs nothing, takes about four hours total, and it is the only way to find out what's actually wrong.**

---

## Why this before distribution

You have no analytics. That was a deliberate choice and the right one — but it means the site is currently a black box. You do not know how long people take, where they hesitate, what they type into the idea box, or whether the recommendation feels right to them.

If you drive a thousand people to it before finding out, you waste the one shot you get with each of those communities on a version that has a fixable flaw in it. Ten people first. Then distribution.

**Do not skip this because it feels slow.** Watching someone use a thing you made is uncomfortable — that discomfort is the actual reason people skip it, not the time cost.

---

## Who to test with

Ten people, aged roughly 16–28, who have **never seen the site**. You need a mix:

| Type | How many | Why they matter |
|---|---|---|
| Genuinely wants to start something, no idea what | 3 | Your core user. If it fails for them it fails. |
| Has a specific idea already | 2 | Tests whether the idea box and industry layer actually land |
| Has never thought about business at all | 2 | Tests whether the landing page earns the four minutes |
| Someone who'll be blunt with you | 2 | You need at least two people who won't be polite |
| Someone over 35 | 1 | Not your target — but they'll spot confusions the young ones absorb silently |

Friends and family are fine. This is not market research, it's usability testing, and for usability testing five to eight people surfaces the large majority of problems.

---

## The setup

**In person is much better than a screen share.** You need to see their face and their thumbs.

Hand them your phone with the site open — **not** your laptop. Most of your traffic will be mobile and mobile is where things break.

Say exactly this, and nothing else:

> "I built this. I want to see someone use it without me explaining anything. Talk out loud while you're doing it — say what you're thinking, even if it's 'this is confusing.' I'm not going to help, and that's not me being rude, it's the whole point. Nothing you say can hurt my feelings."

Then **shut up**.

---

## The hardest rule

**Do not help. Do not explain. Do not defend.**

When they hesitate, you will feel an almost physical urge to say "oh, that just means…". Every time you do that, you delete the data point. Their confusion *is* the finding. If they're stuck for thirty seconds, that's thirty seconds of pure signal — write it down and keep quiet.

If they directly ask you a question, say: *"What do you think it means?"* Then note their answer, because that's what a stranger will assume.

The only time you intervene is if they're about to give up entirely. Then: *"You can skip that one if you want."*

---

## What to write down

Print this or copy it into your notes app. One sheet per person.

```
PERSON #___     Age ___     Their situation: ________________________

□ Did they scroll the landing page before starting, or click straight through?
□ How long from landing to starting the quiz?  ______
□ Total time to finish?  ______

QUESTION-BY-QUESTION — note anything, but especially these:

Q2  THE IDEA BOX
    What did they type, word for word:
    ________________________________________________
    Did they skip it?  Y / N
    Did they hesitate before typing?  Y / N
    Did they look worried about getting it "right"?  Y / N

Q3  INDUSTRY — did they find one that fit, or settle?  ______________
Q4  CAREER  — any reaction? (this one can sting for some people)  ______

Any question they read TWICE:  _________________________________
Any question they asked you about: _____________________________
Any question where they visibly guessed: _______________________

THE RESULT SCREEN
    Their first words on seeing it: ____________________________
    Face: pleased / neutral / disappointed / confused
    Did they read the whole screen or skim to the button?
    Did they say the recommendation felt right?  Y / N
    If no — what did they expect instead, and why?
    ________________________________________________

THE PDF
    Did they download it?  Y / N
    Did they OPEN it?  Y / N          ← this is the one that matters
    Did they scroll past page 2?  Y / N
    Which section did they stop on longest? _____________________
    Any reaction out loud: _____________________________________

AFTERWARDS — ask exactly these four, in this order:
1. "What did you think that was going to do, before you started?"
2. "Was there any point you nearly stopped?"
3. "If you had to delete one question, which?"
4. "Would you actually do anything in that document? Honestly — no is fine."

ONE-LINE SUMMARY of this person:
________________________________________________________________
```

---

## The specific things I'd bet money on

These are the parts most likely to break, because they're the newest and least tested. Watch them closely:

**The idea box (Q2).** This is the single biggest unknown in the product. It's the only place a user can do something unpredictable. Expect some of:
- People skipping it because they feel put on the spot
- People typing one word ("food") instead of a description
- People typing something that contradicts the industry they then pick
- People treating it like a search box and expecting a result

If **more than half skip it**, the question is either intimidating or in the wrong place. The likely fix is moving it later, after they've built momentum — question 2 is early to ask someone to produce a thought.

**The career question (Q4).** For someone unemployed or in a job they're ashamed of, this can land badly. Watch their face. If it reads as judgement rather than as "this is an asset", the framing needs work.

**The result.** If someone gets a recommendation and their face falls, that's the most valuable thirty seconds of the whole test. Find out what they expected. That's a scoring weight, and it's a small fix.

**Whether they open the PDF.** Downloading is polite. Opening is interest. Reading past page two is the real signal. If people download and never open, the problem is the results screen overpromising or the PDF not feeling urgent.

---

## Reading the results

After all ten, look for **patterns, not opinions.**

- **One person struggled with something** → probably that person. Note it, move on.
- **Three or more hit the same thing** → that is a real defect. Fix it.
- **Everyone did something you didn't expect** → your model of the user is wrong, and that's the most valuable finding available.

Rank the fixes by how many people hit them, not by how easy they are to fix or how much you agree.

Then change **one thing at a time**, and note what you changed. If you fix six things at once you'll never know which one mattered.

---

## The uncomfortable question

At the end of the ten, answer this honestly in writing:

> **Did anyone say they would actually do something in the document?**

If the answer is nobody, the site is a well-built thing that doesn't change behaviour, and no amount of distribution fixes that. That would be worth knowing after ten conversations rather than after ten thousand visitors.

If two or three say yes, you have something. Go to the distribution kit.

---

## Timeboxing this

Ten people at ~20 minutes each, including the conversation afterwards, is about **three and a half hours** of your time — spread over a week or two, since you'll be catching people when you can.

Set a deadline. Two weeks from today. If you've only managed six by then, six is enough — six is where most of the findings live anyway. Don't let the perfect ten become the reason you never start distribution.
